# Audiovisual Media Services Directive

## A plain-English breakdown for streaming and television

**Prepared for Double FW Ltd.**  
**Review date: 1 November 2026.**  
**Requested source: Directive (EU) 2018/1808.**

This guide explains accessibility and the other main rules affecting television, on-demand streaming and qualifying video-sharing platforms.

It follows the plain-English approach used in the accompanying EAA and public sector web accessibility guides.

### Understand which law you are reading

Directive **2018/1808** changes the existing **Audiovisual Media Services Directive, 2010/13/EU**.

It is an amending law, rather than a separate law dealing only with streaming.

For practical use, read its changes alongside the amended 2010/13/EU text.

The EUR-Lex consolidated version dated **8 February 2025** includes a later change made by the European Media Freedom Act. [S1–S3]

**Article references in this guide normally refer to the amended 2010/13/EU Directive.**

For example, the accessibility duty is **Article 7 of 2010/13/EU**, replaced by **Article 1(11) of 2018/1808**.

### How to use this guide

- **Legal requirement** explains what the EU legislation requires countries to secure through their national systems.
- **Practical check** recommends a way to assess an outcome.
- **Evidence** means records supporting a finding or report.

Practical checks do not create new legal quotas or certification rules.

National laws, regulator rules, licence conditions and service classifications determine the detailed duties for a particular provider.

This guide explains the EU framework. It does not reproduce each country's broadcasting or on-demand rules, including the UK's current arrangements.

## 1. Purpose and intended outcomes

**Legal basis: Directive 2018/1808 and the amended AVMSD. [S1, S2]**

The Directive updates EU audiovisual media rules for changes in how people produce, distribute and watch video.

Its purposes include:

- Improving disabled people's access to audiovisual services.
- Protecting children from harmful content.
- Protecting audiences from incitement to violence or hatred and specified criminal content.
- Making commercial content recognisable and subject to safeguards.
- Supporting European works and cultural diversity.
- Bringing qualifying video-sharing platforms within specific rules.
- Supporting independent regulation and cooperation between countries.
- Developing media literacy.

The rules differ between editorial media services and video-sharing platforms.

They also distinguish scheduled television from on-demand services.

**A streaming service is not automatically subject to every television rule.**

## 2. Who the Directive supports

**Legal references: Articles 6, 6a, 7, 9 and 28b; recitals 22–24 of 2018/1808. [S1, S2]**

The wider Directive supports audiences generally.

Specific provisions support disabled people, children, consumers and people affected by harmful or unlawful content.

### Accessibility

Article 7 concerns **persons with disabilities**.

The recitals particularly discuss people with visual or hearing impairments.

Relevant audiences include:

- Blind and partially sighted people.
- Deaf and hard-of-hearing people.
- Sign language users.
- People with combined sight and hearing loss.
- Disabled people whose reading, language, movement or other access needs affect their use of audiovisual services.

The Article 7 wording is not limited to a closed list of diagnoses.

Accessibility features can also help older people and viewers with temporary or situational needs.

Examples include watching without sound or needing support to understand unfamiliar speech.

### Different features meet different needs

Subtitles, sign language and audio description do not provide interchangeable experiences.

For example, written subtitles may not provide equivalent access for someone whose first language is a sign language.

Audio description explains relevant visual information. It does not replace the dialogue and sound that a viewer cannot hear.

Plan evaluation around the person's access needs and the information required to follow the programme.

Do not assume that one available feature makes every programme accessible to every disabled viewer.

## 3. Which services are covered

**Legal references: Article 1(1)(a), (aa), (b), (c), (d), (e) and (g). [S1, S2]**

### 3.1 Editorial audiovisual media services

A qualifying service provides programmes to the general public to inform, entertain or educate through electronic communications networks.

The programmes are under a provider's **editorial responsibility**.

That means effective control over programme selection and organisation into a schedule or catalogue.

Providing programmes must be the service's principal purpose, or the principal purpose of a separately identifiable part of it.

The definition also concerns a service within the relevant EU Treaty meaning of economic activity.

It does not depend solely on whether viewers pay a subscription.

### 3.2 Scheduled television

This is a **linear service**: the provider organises a schedule for simultaneous viewing.

It can include scheduled television delivered through the internet, rather than only terrestrial, cable or satellite television.

### 3.3 On-demand services

These let viewers choose programmes from the provider's catalogue and watch them at their chosen time.

Examples can include subscription video libraries, catch-up television and transactional video services.

Advertising-funded or free-to-view services can also qualify.

### 3.4 Video-sharing platforms

A qualifying platform provides programmes, user-generated videos, or both, to the public without holding editorial responsibility for that content.

The platform determines how content is organised, including through algorithms, display, tagging or sequencing.

Video provision can be:

- The principal purpose of the service.
- The principal purpose of a separately identifiable section.
- An essential function of the service.

This can bring some social media services within the platform provisions.

It does not mean that every social network or website containing a video is automatically a video-sharing platform.

Commission guidance explains the essential-function assessment. The competent authority considers the actual service. [S4]

### 3.5 Mixed services and creator channels

One business can provide both an editorial catalogue and a video-sharing platform.

A professional channel or creator service may itself need assessment as an audiovisual media service.

Hosting a channel on another platform does not automatically settle the channel provider's legal classification.

Assess the activity, public audience, economic character, editorial control and applicable jurisdiction.

### 3.6 Commercial communications

The framework also regulates audiovisual advertising and other audiovisual commercial communications.

These can include sponsorship, product placement and qualifying advertising within relevant services.

## 4. Which country regulates a service

**Legal references: Articles 2–4 and 28a. [S2]**

The AVMSD generally uses a **country-of-origin** approach.

The responsible EU country is determined through the Directive's jurisdiction rules.

### Editorial media services

Relevant factors include:

- The provider's head office.
- Where editorial decisions are made.
- Where the relevant workforce operates.
- Additional establishment rules where those factors are split.
- Specified satellite connections where the establishment rules do not resolve jurisdiction.

### Video-sharing platforms

These have their own establishment rules under Article 28a.

In specified cases, an EU parent company, subsidiary or group undertaking can establish the relevant EU connection.

### Important limits

Having EU viewers does not by itself establish AVMSD jurisdiction over every overseas service.

Being registered outside the EU does not by itself settle the issue either.

Article 13 separately permits certain financial contribution duties for services targeting another country's audience.

That provision is not a general transfer of every regulatory duty to each audience country.

Record the relevant national regulator and legal basis. Obtain a specific jurisdiction assessment where the organisation or service crosses borders.

Countries can introduce stricter or more detailed rules within the framework's conditions and other EU law.

## 5. What is outside scope or treated differently

**Legal basis: Article 1 definitions; Articles 2, 7, 13 and 28a–28b. [S1, S2]**

### 5.1 Not every internet service is an audiovisual media service

Activities that may fall outside the relevant definition include:

- Private correspondence and personal video calls.
- Purely private, non-economic video sharing by individuals.
- Audio-only radio or podcast services.
- Websites where video is only incidental to another activity.
- Closed internal video systems that do not serve the general public.

These are scope examples, not universal exemptions for every service using those descriptions.

Assess a separately identifiable video section or an essential video function where relevant.

Other accessibility, communications, employment, platform or consumer laws can still apply.

### 5.2 Small providers

The AVMSD does not give every small media service a blanket accessibility exemption.

Article 7 requires proportionate measures. National rules determine the detailed application.

The low-turnover and low-audience exceptions in Article 13 concern specified **European works obligations**.

They are not a general exemption from accessibility, child protection or advertising duties.

Do not import the EAA's microenterprise service exemption into Article 7.

### 5.3 Live programmes

Recital 22 recognises practical constraints, including real-time programmes and events.

It does not create a blanket exclusion for live audiovisual services.

Do not apply the WAD's live-media exclusion to an AVMSD service.

National rules may distinguish live and pre-recorded provision. Record the relevant rule and the actual constraints.

### 5.4 Public service broadcasters

Public service broadcasting can fall within the AVMSD.

The WAD's exclusion for public service broadcaster websites and apps does not remove AVMSD audiovisual duties.

### 5.5 Video-sharing platforms and accessibility

The platform-specific rules in Articles 28a–28b do not themselves extend Article 7 to every user-uploaded video.

A platform may also operate an editorial audiovisual service to which Article 7 applies.

Other legislation, including relevant EAA or national requirements, needs separate assessment.

Do not tell a platform that Article 7 automatically requires subtitles or audio description for every upload.

## 6. The accessibility duties in Article 7

**Legal reference: Article 7(1)–(5), introduced by Article 1(11) of 2018/1808. [S1, S2]**

### 6.1 Continuous, progressive improvement

Countries must ensure, without undue delay, that services under their jurisdiction become continuously and progressively more accessible through proportionate measures.

This is an ongoing improvement duty.

It is not satisfied merely by publishing an ambition with no implemented progress.

### 6.2 Regular provider reporting

Countries must ensure that providers regularly report implementation of their accessibility measures to national regulatory authorities or bodies.

The EU provision does not set one identical provider reporting interval for every country and service.

Check the national regulator's reporting requirements.

### 6.3 National reports to the Commission

Countries must report implementation of the improvement duty to the Commission.

The first deadline was **19 December 2022**, followed by reports every three years.

This is different from the provider's regular reporting duty.

### 6.4 Accessibility action plans

Countries must encourage providers to develop action plans for continuous, progressive improvement.

Any such plan must be communicated to the national regulatory authority or body.

Article 7(3) is an encouragement duty on countries. It does not itself impose an identical mandatory action-plan template on every provider.

National law or regulator rules may impose more specific requirements.

### 6.5 An accessible national contact point

Each country must designate a single publicly available online contact point for accessibility information and complaints.

That contact point must be easily accessible, including to disabled people.

It is not the same as a universal requirement for every provider to use the WAD's model accessibility statement.

### 6.6 Accessible emergency information

Countries must ensure that emergency information made public through audiovisual media services is accessible to disabled people.

This includes public communications and announcements during natural disasters.

See Section 10 for practical assessment.

### What Article 7 does not specify

It does not set:

- One EU-wide subtitle percentage.
- One EU-wide audio-description percentage.
- One mandatory percentage of sign-language programmes.
- One universal caption accuracy score or delay limit.
- A common deadline for making every programme fully accessible.
- A WAD-style model statement for every streaming provider.
- An Article 6-style presumption of conformity based on one harmonised standard.

National rules can set quotas, quality measures, reporting methods and other detailed conditions.

**The 30% figure elsewhere in the AVMSD concerns European works. It is not an accessibility quota.**

## 7. What accessible viewing should achieve

**Legal context: Article 7; recital 23. The outcome descriptions below are practical recommendations. [S1, S2]**

Recital 23 identifies sign language, subtitles for Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, spoken subtitles and audio description as accessibility methods.

These examples are not an exhaustive list.

### 7.1 Subtitles for Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers

**Practical outcome:** viewers can follow relevant speech and meaningful sound information.

Assessment should consider dialogue, speaker identification, important sounds, timing, readability and completeness.

Translation subtitles alone may omit information needed by someone who cannot hear the soundtrack.

### 7.2 Audio description

**Practical outcome:** viewers can obtain important visual information that the existing soundtrack does not communicate adequately.

Assessment should consider actions, identity, setting, relevant on-screen text and narrative information.

Description should fit the programme's purpose and tone while preserving important dialogue and sound.

A programme that communicates its relevant information fully through sound may need a different approach from visually driven drama.

That editorial judgement does not override a national quota or programme-specific requirement.

### 7.3 Sign language

**Practical outcome:** intended sign language users can follow the programme through an understandable and visible signed presentation.

Assess language suitability, visibility, framing, timing and whether interface elements obscure the signer.

Do not assume that text subtitles provide the same access.

### 7.4 Spoken subtitles

**Practical outcome:** people who cannot read displayed subtitles can obtain their information through speech where this feature is provided.

Check timing, intelligibility and interaction with the existing soundtrack or audio description.

### 7.5 Complete service delivery

An access file existing in a production system does not establish that the viewer received it.

Check the distributed version, programme language, device and delivery route.

The AVMSD content duties and separate EAA access-service duties need distinct legal records, even when tested through the same viewing journey.

## 8. Measuring accessibility and progress

**Legal basis: Article 7(1)–(3). The measurement framework below is recommended practice, subject to national requirements. [S1, S2]**

### 8.1 Establish the applicable legal baseline

Record:

- Service classification and jurisdiction.
- National law, regulator rules and licence conditions.
- Applicable quotas, quality requirements and reporting dates.
- Any permitted adjustments, exceptions or phased requirements.
- The reporting period and calculation method.

Use the regulator's method where one is prescribed.

An internal indicator should not silently replace a statutory measure.

### 8.2 Measure provision separately for each feature

Useful indicators include:

- Programme hours or minutes with subtitles.
- Programme hours or minutes with audio description.
- Programme hours or minutes with sign language.
- Provision of spoken subtitles where relevant.
- Availability across languages, devices and delivery routes.

Do not combine these into one figure suggesting every viewer benefits equally from every feature.

### 8.3 State the denominator

For example:

**Subtitle coverage = qualifying programme minutes delivered with subtitles ÷ total qualifying programme minutes × 100.**

This is an illustrative measure. Apply the national method if it specifies another denominator or counting rule.

Record how the calculation treats:

- Episodes, series and individual titles.
- Repeats and duplicate catalogue entries.
- Live, catch-up and on-demand versions.
- Different languages and regional versions.
- Advertising, trailers and other non-programme material.
- Acquired and original content.
- Failed, missing or unusable access tracks.

Do not improve the reported figure by silently removing difficult programmes from the denominator.

### 8.4 Measure change over time

Keep a dated baseline and comparable reporting periods.

Report what changed, which users benefit, and which barriers remain.

If the catalogue or calculation changes, explain how that affects comparison.

For example, moving from 60% to 70% subtitle coverage is a ten-percentage-point increase under that measure.

Those example values are not legal targets.

Absolute provision can rise while the percentage falls if the catalogue grows. Report enough information to explain that situation.

### 8.5 Check quality

Feature availability alone is insufficient to understand the viewing experience.

Recommended quality checks include:

- Accuracy and completeness of subtitle information.
- Subtitle synchronisation and readable presentation.
- Audio-description relevance, timing and intelligibility.
- Sign-language visibility and understandable delivery.
- Spoken-subtitle intelligibility and synchronisation.
- Correct language and programme version.
- Playback reliability throughout the programme.

Use any nationally prescribed quality method and thresholds.

Where none exists, define an appropriate assessment method and label it as an organisational quality measure.

The AVMSD does not supply one universal numerical threshold for each of these checks.

### 8.6 Test actual viewing journeys

Recommended tasks include:

1. Find a programme with the required feature.
2. Identify whether the feature matches the viewer's needs and language.
3. Start playback and activate it.
4. Pause, seek and resume.
5. Check the feature after an advertisement or episode change.
6. Confirm that important information remains understandable.
7. Report a missing or defective feature.

Catalogue and control checks can support both quality assurance and separate EAA assessment where that law applies.

Do not label every interface check as an express Article 7 technical requirement.

### 8.7 Evaluate with disabled viewers

Recruit viewers with the access needs and language preferences relevant to the feature.

Include different hearing, vision and combined needs where relevant.

Ask whether they could understand key events, identify speakers and follow the intended information or story.

Record comprehension, effort, assistance, feature failures, confidence and satisfaction.

The EU provision does not prescribe a fixed participant number.

A small qualitative study can identify barriers. It cannot establish a population-wide accessibility percentage.

### 8.8 Separate the conclusions

| Conclusion | What it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Legal applicability | Which service rules and national requirements apply? |
| Provision | How much relevant content has each feature? |
| Quality and delivery | Does the correct feature work through the assessed delivery route? |
| Viewer experience | Can intended viewers understand and follow the content? |
| Progress | What improved against the documented baseline? |

Link these conclusions through evidence. Do not collapse them into a single compliance score.

A satisfied participant does not cancel a missed national quota.

A quota being met does not by itself demonstrate that the supplied access features work well.

### Keep decisions accurate

Use statuses such as **met**, **not met**, **not assessed** and **not applicable**, with evidence and reasons.

Record any national exception or proportionality decision separately from a technical result.

Do not count an untested feature as working or an unavailable feature as technically compliant.

### Example

The production master contains audio description, but the streamed version has no selectable description track.

Record the production asset as present and the assessed delivery route as failing to provide it.

Investigate packaging, metadata, distribution and player configuration.

Retest the actual streamed programme after correction.

## 10. Emergency information

**Legal reference: Article 7(5); recital 24. [S1, S2]**

Emergency information made public through audiovisual media services must be provided accessibly to disabled people.

It includes public communications and announcements in natural disaster situations.

### Practical outcome

People should receive the essential information and understand the required action through an accessible presentation.

Do not assess only whether the announcement was transmitted.

Check whether relevant audiences could obtain the message, instructions, locations and updates.

### Practical checks

Use controlled exercises to assess:

- Accessible presentation of the essential message.
- Subtitles, speech, signing or other relevant methods.
- Timing and synchronisation of updates.
- Conflicting or missing information across versions.
- Responsibility for urgent correction.

Where several laws apply, also assess the access interface and delivery system under their respective requirements.

### Evidence

Keep message versions, transmission times, accessible variants, exercise findings and corrective actions.

Recital 24 recognises exceptional cases where accessible provision may not be possible and says these should not prevent emergency information being made public.

That recital does not create a general exemption from planning accessible emergency communication.

## 11. Protecting children and other audiences

**Legal references: Articles 6, 6a and 9. [S1, S2]**

### 11.1 Incitement and specified criminal content

Audiovisual services must not contain the incitement to violence or hatred prohibited by Article 6.

The provision refers to protected grounds in Article 21 of the EU Charter, including disability.

It also addresses public provocation to commit terrorist offences under the specified EU law.

This is different from treating every challenging, offensive or distressing portrayal as legally prohibited.

### 11.2 Potentially harmful content for children

Countries must ensure potentially harmful services are available in ways that prevent children from normally seeing or hearing the harmful content.

Measures can include scheduling, age verification and other technical controls.

They must be proportionate to the potential harm.

The most harmful content, such as pornography and gratuitous violence, requires the strictest measures.

Providers must supply sufficient information describing the potentially harmful nature of content.

### 11.3 Children's data

Children's personal data collected or generated through Article 6a protection measures must not be used for commercial purposes.

The prohibition includes direct marketing, profiling and behaviourally targeted advertising.

### Practical assessment

Check classification, warnings, access controls, applicable scheduling, complaint handling and the use of data generated by protective measures.

Test whether disabled users can operate relevant controls and obtain warnings without losing access to permitted content.

That accessibility check supports usable protection. Assess its legal basis alongside the applicable accessibility laws.

### Evidence

Retain classification rules, control tests, warning examples, relevant data-flow records and corrective actions.

## 12. Video-sharing platform duties

**Legal references: Articles 28a–28b. [S1, S2]**

Qualifying platforms must take appropriate measures to protect:

- Children from content that may harm their development.
- The public from prohibited incitement to violence or hatred.
- The public from the specified criminal content.

The criminal-content provisions concern terrorist provocation, child sexual abuse material, and specified racist or xenophobic offences.

### 12.1 Measures appropriate to the platform

Depending on the circumstances, Article 28b measures include:

- Suitable terms and conditions and their application.
- Tools for uploaders to declare commercial content.
- Transparent, user-friendly reporting and flagging.
- Information explaining the result of a report.
- Age-verification systems where appropriate.
- Content-rating systems.
- Parental controls for relevant content.
- Transparent and effective complaint handling.
- Media-literacy measures and user awareness.

Measures must be practicable and proportionate, considering the platform's size, service and risks.

They must respect relevant rights and EU legal limits on monitoring and filtering.

Do not interpret the provision as an unlimited requirement to pre-approve every upload.

### 12.2 Commercial content

Platforms have stronger direct duties for commercial communications they market, sell or arrange.

They must take appropriate measures for other commercial communications, taking account of their limited control.

Users must be clearly informed of commercial communications where the platform receives the relevant declaration or knows they are present.

### 12.3 Complaints and review

Countries must arrange assessment of whether platform measures are appropriate.

Out-of-court redress must be available for relevant disputes, without removing access to judicial remedies under the applicable provisions.

Children's data collected through the relevant protection measures must not be used commercially.

### Practical evidence

Retain the policy version, reporting tools, moderation procedures, complaint outcomes, access-control tests and effectiveness assessments.

An algorithm or moderation policy existing on paper does not demonstrate that reports receive an effective response.

The Digital Services Act and other laws may impose additional duties. They require their own assessment.

## 13. Advertising, sponsorship and product placement

**Legal references: Articles 9–11, 19–20, 23 and 28b(2). [S1, S2]**

### 13.1 General commercial safeguards

Audiovisual commercial communications must be recognisable.

Surreptitious advertising and subliminal techniques are prohibited.

Commercial communications must respect human dignity and the relevant discrimination and protection rules.

Specific restrictions address tobacco and related products, prescription medicines, alcohol, and harm to children.

The precise conditions differ by product, format and service type.

Check the full applicable provision rather than assuming one rule applies to every kind of commercial communication.

### 13.2 Sponsorship

Sponsorship must meet identification and editorial-independence conditions.

There are restrictions on who may sponsor and which programmes may be sponsored.

Check the programme type and sponsor category under Article 10 and national law.

### 13.3 Product placement

Article 11 permits product placement subject to conditions and specified programme exclusions.

The exclusions include news and current affairs, consumer affairs, religious and children's programmes.

Other conditions concern editorial independence, promotion, prominence and viewer identification.

Article 11 applies to programmes produced after 19 December 2009. Check its detailed conditions and national rules.

### 13.4 Television advertising time

Article 23 limits television advertising and teleshopping spots to:

- **20% of the period from 06:00 to 18:00.**
- **20% of the period from 18:00 to 24:00.**

Specified announcements, sponsorship announcements, product placement and neutral separating frames are excluded from that calculation.

Separate rules govern advertising interruptions, including particular conditions for films, news, children's programmes and religious services.

**Do not apply the television 20% time rule automatically to an on-demand catalogue.**

It is also unrelated to subtitle or audio-description coverage.

### Practical evidence

Keep commercial-content identification records, sponsor and placement reviews, transmission logs and applicable time calculations.

For on-demand services, assess the relevant commercial-content rules rather than copying a television scheduling checklist.

## 14. European works and prominence

**Legal references: Articles 1(1)(n), 7a and 13. Commission guidance: [S5].**

### 14.1 On-demand catalogue share

Covered on-demand providers must secure at least **30% European works** in their catalogues and ensure those works receive prominence.

European works have a legal definition. The category is not limited simply to productions made in current EU countries.

Use the legal definition and the applicable national counting method.

Commission guidance supports calculation and explains relevant low-audience and low-turnover concepts. The guidance itself is not binding.

### 14.2 Prominence

Catalogue presence alone does not address the separate prominence duty.

Assess how European works are presented and made discoverable under the applicable rules.

### 14.3 Financial contributions

Countries may require financial contributions to European works through investment or national funds.

Within the specified conditions, they may also impose proportionate, non-discriminatory contributions on services established elsewhere in the EU that target their audiences.

The framework includes safeguards concerning the revenue basis and contributions in other countries.

### 14.4 Exceptions

Article 13(6) excludes providers with low turnover or low audiences from its specified obligations.

Countries may also waive relevant duties where the service's nature or theme makes them impracticable or unjustified.

Record which obligation, rule and evidence support the exception.

These exceptions do not automatically remove Article 7 accessibility responsibilities.

### 14.5 General-interest services

Article 7a separately allows countries to take measures ensuring appropriate prominence for audiovisual services of general interest.

This differs from the Article 13 duty concerning European works within an on-demand catalogue.

### Practical evidence

Retain catalogue lists, work-classification evidence, counting rules, prominence assessment, revenue records where relevant and any justified exception.

Keep European-work calculations separate from accessibility provision figures.

## 15. Provider information, programme integrity and media literacy

**Legal references: Articles 4a, 5, 7b and 33a. [S1, S2]**

### 15.1 Provider information

Providers must make specified information easily, directly and permanently available.

This includes their name, geographical address, effective contact details, jurisdiction country and relevant regulator or supervisory body.

Under Article 5(2), countries may require ownership information subject to stated conditions.

Separate current ownership-transparency duties may arise under the European Media Freedom Act. [S3]

### 15.2 Programme integrity

Countries must take appropriate, proportionate measures against unauthorised commercial overlays or modifications of audiovisual services.

National rules must address the relevant exceptions and legitimate interests.

Recital 26 distinguishes necessary controls, legitimate subtitles, warnings and private user-authorised overlays from the targeted commercial interference.

Do not present Article 7b as a general prohibition on accessibility features or user customisation.

### 15.3 Codes of conduct

The Directive supports co-regulation and self-regulation under specified conditions.

Relevant codes need clear objectives, monitoring and effective enforcement arrangements.

A voluntary commitment is not automatically equivalent to satisfying a statutory duty.

### 15.4 Media literacy

Countries must promote and take measures to develop media-literacy skills.

These help people understand media, recognise commercial or harmful content, and make informed choices.

Countries report implementation to the Commission every three years, starting from 19 December 2022.

### Practical evidence

Check provider details, programme-integrity controls, relevant code assessments, education measures and their effectiveness.

## 16. Responsibilities through the production and delivery process

**The allocation below is recommended practice. The regulated provider remains responsible under the applicable national framework.**

| Role | Practical responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Accountable provider or service owner | Establish scope, national duties, resources and regulatory reporting. |
| Commissioning and acquisition | Specify access assets, languages, rights, delivery requirements and quality expectations. |
| Production and editorial teams | Plan meaningful accessibility alongside programme creation. |
| Access-service suppliers | Create and review subtitles, description, signing or spoken subtitles. |
| Distribution and platform engineering | Preserve correct assets, synchronisation and metadata through delivery. |
| Product and design teams | Make relevant access features discoverable and usable under applicable service-access duties. |
| QA and research | Test actual programme versions and viewing journeys with relevant audiences. |
| Support and complaints teams | Receive problems, provide useful information and track correction. |
| Compliance and reporting | Maintain evidence, calculate provision and track progressive improvement. |

Contracts should identify who corrects errors at each stage.

An acquired programme's access track should not be assumed unavailable without checking rights and supplied assets.

Conversely, do not promise a feature that is not licensed, delivered or operational in the actual service.

### Recommended action-plan contents

Include:

- Current provision and known barriers.
- Intended audience outcomes.
- Applicable legal targets and separate organisational targets.
- Priorities, responsibilities and resources.
- Delivery dates and dependencies.
- Quality and user evaluation methods.
- Progress reporting and review arrangements.

Label proposed targets clearly. Do not present an internal ambition as an EU statutory quota.

## 17. Reporting, regulators and enforcement

**Legal references: Articles 7, 30 and 30a; later institutional change in Regulation 2024/1083. [S2, S3]**

Countries implement the Directive through national law and regulatory arrangements.

Article 30 addresses independent national audiovisual regulators, resources, powers and appeal mechanisms.

Detailed enforcement measures, sanctions and provider response requirements depend on the national framework.

The Directive does not set one identical EU-wide fine for an accessibility failure.

### Provider accessibility reporting

Check the regulator's required format, calculation rules, scope and timetable.

Retain the evidence supporting reported coverage and progress.

Explain significant changes in catalogue size, service scope or measurement method.

### Complaints

Identify the national Article 7 online contact point and relevant provider complaint route.

Test whether people can use the route and understand the response.

Do not invent a universal AVMSD response deadline where national rules supply the detail.

### Current European cooperation

The 2018 text referred to the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services, known as ERGA.

The European Media Freedom Act subsequently deleted AVMSD Article 30b and introduced the European Board for Media Services arrangements.

That change applied from **8 February 2025**. Do not describe the original Article 30b structure as the unchanged current framework.

## 19. How the AVMSD, EAA and WAD fit together

**Legal sources: AVMSD [S1, S2]; EAA [S6]; WAD [S7].**

| Framework | Main concern in this comparison |
| --- | --- |
| AVMSD | Audiovisual service and content rules, including progressive accessibility under Article 7. |
| EAA | Covered products and services providing access to audiovisual media, including relevant interfaces, programme guides and supplied access-feature delivery. |
| WAD | Websites and apps of qualifying public sector bodies, subject to its exclusions, including the public service broadcaster exclusion. |

### Example: an on-demand programme

**Content provision:** assess the applicable AVMSD and national requirements for subtitles, audio description, signing or other access measures.

**Service access:** separately assess applicable EAA duties for finding, selecting and using the audiovisual service and its supplied accessibility components.

**Public sector website:** assess WAD applicability where relevant, including the body's status and specific exclusions.

One end-to-end test can reveal failures across these areas.

However, retain separate legal references and conclusions. A single WCAG score cannot stand in for all three frameworks.

### Standards and quality methods

WCAG, relevant technical standards and editorial quality guides can support particular assessments.

Their use does not create an automatic Article 7 presumption of conformity or replace national broadcasting rules.

The AVMSD does not contain the EAA's Annex I functional performance criteria or the WAD's model statement decision.

## 21. Suggested Next Steps

1. Classify each service as scheduled television, on-demand media, a video-sharing platform, or another activity.
2. Establish jurisdiction and identify the relevant national regulator.
3. Record national accessibility, content, advertising and other applicable rules.
4. Establish provision and quality baselines for each relevant access feature.
5. Define measurable improvement actions and preserve their supporting evidence.
6. Test the delivered programmes and relevant viewing journeys, including emergency information arrangements.
7. Evaluate with disabled viewers whose needs match the features being assessed.
8. Correct failures and measure whether the changes improved access.
9. Report through the required national process and communicate any Article 7 action plan.
10. Assess EAA, WAD and other legal duties separately where they apply.

## 22. Glossary and sources

### Short glossary

**Access feature:** a feature helping people obtain programme information, such as subtitles, audio description or sign language.

**Audio description:** spoken information explaining relevant visual content not adequately communicated by the existing soundtrack.

**Audiovisual:** involving moving images, with or without sound, in the relevant media context.

**Catalogue:** the collection of programmes offered for on-demand selection.

**Commercial communication:** audiovisual material promoting goods, services or a commercial identity under the legal definition.

**Consolidated text:** a reading version combining a law and later changes. The authentic published legal acts remain authoritative.

**Editorial responsibility:** effective control over programme selection and organisation into a schedule or catalogue.

**Jurisdiction:** the legal basis determining which country's rules and regulator apply.

**Linear service:** a scheduled service watched simultaneously by viewers.

**Media literacy:** skills supporting informed use, understanding and evaluation of media.

**On-demand service:** a service where viewers choose programmes and viewing times from a provider's catalogue.

**Prominence:** how content or services are presented and made noticeable or discoverable under the relevant rule.

**Proportionate measures:** measures appropriate to the legal objective and relevant circumstances, as applied through the national framework.

**Spoken subtitles:** subtitle information presented through speech.

**Video-sharing platform:** a service meeting the legal platform definition, including the relevant video function and organisation conditions.

### Limits and maintenance

This guide summarises the 2018 reforms and explains their practical accessibility implications within the amended framework.

It is not a complete national broadcasting code or an exhaustive account of every original AVMSD provision.

The suggested evidence measures support assessment. They do not certify a service or replace regulator requirements.

Review national law, regulator rules and later adopted legislation before making compliance claims.
